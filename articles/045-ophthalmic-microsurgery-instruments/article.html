Ophthalmic instruments are some of the finest surgical instruments made. Eye surgery works at a very small scale, often under a microscope, so tips are tiny, springs are light and tolerances are tight. This introduction covers the main instrument types used in ophthalmic microsurgery.

In short

Ophthalmic sets are built around spring-handled micro scissors and needle holders, very fine forceps, eyelid specula and calipers. Their delicate tips need careful handling and dedicated reprocessing.

Spring handles

Most ophthalmic micro instruments have spring handles and are held like a pencil. This allows small, precise movements with the fingers, with less hand movement than ring-handled instruments. Handles are often round or textured so they can be rolled between the fingers.

Tips and tolerances

Ophthalmic forceps tips can be a fraction of a millimetre wide, and scissors blades are only a few millimetres long. Even small damage, such as a bent tip, makes an instrument unusable. Tip protectors, instrument mats and careful handling are essential.

Caring for ophthalmic instruments

Care checklist

  • Handle instruments individually; never pile them in a basin.
  • Protect tips with silicone tip guards.
  • Use dedicated trays with holders for each instrument.
  • Inspect tips under magnification after every use.
  • Follow cleaning instructions carefully; residues are a particular concern in eye surgery.

Frequently asked questions

Why are ophthalmic instruments spring-handled?

Spring handles allow fine finger control and small movements, which suits microsurgery.

What is the difference between Vannas and Westcott scissors?

Both are micro spring scissors. Vannas scissors are very small and fine; Westcott scissors are slightly larger and are often used for conjunctival work.

Can ophthalmic instruments be repaired?

Some can be realigned or sharpened by specialist repair services, but very fine tips that are badly bent usually need replacement.
