Surgical Scissors Explained: Mayo, Metzenbaum, Iris and More

Scissors are among the most used instruments in any theatre, clinic or ward, and also among the most misused. A pair designed for delicate tissue will dull quickly if it is used on sutures or dressings, and a heavy pair is clumsy in fine work. Knowing the main patterns makes it much easier to order the right scissors and keep them in good condition for longer.

In short

Choose the pattern for the tissue (heavy, delicate or fine), then choose straight or curved, the tips, the length and the edge. Keep tissue scissors for tissue, and give sutures and dressings their own scissors.

The main patterns

Mayo scissors

Mayo scissors are sturdy, with relatively short, broad blades. They are used for heavier tissue such as fascia and muscle. Straight Mayo scissors are traditionally used to cut sutures, while curved Mayo scissors are used for dissecting thicker tissue.

Metzenbaum scissors

Metzenbaum ("Metz") scissors have long handles and comparatively short, slim blades. That combination gives reach and control for delicate tissue and blunt dissection deep in a wound. They should not be used on sutures, drapes or dressings, which dull their fine edges.

Iris scissors

Iris scissors are small and very sharp, with fine tips. They were originally made for eye surgery but are now used across fine plastic, dermatological and general work where precision matters more than strength.

Stitch and suture-removal scissors

Patterns such as Spencer and Littauer stitch scissors have a small hook or notch on one blade that slips under a suture so it can be lifted and cut cleanly. They are a staple in clinics and outpatient departments.

Bandage and dressing scissors

Lister bandage scissors have an angled design and a blunt, flattened tip on the lower blade that slides under dressings without injuring the skin. Universal or "tough cut" scissors are made for cutting gauze, tape and other materials.

Straight or curved?

Straight blades give a clean, predictable cut and are the usual choice for sutures and superficial work. Curved blades let the user see the tips and follow tissue planes, which is why most dissecting scissors are curved. Many departments stock both versions of their most used pattern.

Tips: sharp, blunt or one of each

  • Sharp/sharp: both tips pointed, for precise cuts and fine work.
  • Blunt/blunt: both tips rounded, safer for dissection near structures you do not want to pierce.
  • Sharp/blunt: one of each, a common all-purpose choice for dressings and general use.

Length

Scissors are ordered by overall length, usually in centimetres (often with the inch equivalent). Shorter scissors (around 9 to 12 cm) suit fine and superficial work; longer ones (18 cm and above) reach deeper into a wound. The same pattern is often available in several lengths, so always state the length when you order.

Edges: standard, serrated, "supercut" and tungsten carbide

  • Standard edges suit most work and can be resharpened.
  • Micro-serrated edges grip tissue and stop it sliding out of the blades.
  • Supercut scissors (often with black handles) have one razor-sharp blade for a very smooth, precise cut.
  • Tungsten carbide (TC) inserts (gold handles) stay sharp much longer than plain steel and are a good investment for high-use patterns.

Checking a new pair

Quick quality check

  • The blades open and close smoothly, with even tension and no grinding.
  • The scissors cut cleanly along the full length of the blades, right to the tips.
  • The tips meet exactly when closed and do not cross or gap.
  • The screw is flush and secure, with no play in the joint.
  • The finish is smooth and even, without burrs where tissue could catch.

Making scissors last

Most damage comes from using scissors for the wrong job. Keep delicate scissors for tissue only, protect the tips with tip guards during reprocessing, clean them in the open position, and lubricate the joint with a water-soluble instrument lubricant. Have sharp instruments professionally resharpened when they start to chew rather than cut.
