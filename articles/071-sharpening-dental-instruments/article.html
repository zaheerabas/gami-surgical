Scalers and curettes lose their edge with every use. A dull instrument burnishes calculus instead of removing it, needs more force and makes appointments longer and less comfortable. Regularly sharpening dental instruments keeps them efficient and extends their working life.

In short

Test instruments regularly, sharpen them before they become very dull, keep the original blade angles, and replace instruments whose blades have become thin.

How to tell when an instrument is dull

  • Test stick: draw the edge lightly across a plastic test stick. A sharp edge bites; a dull edge slides.
  • Light test: under good light, a sharp edge is a fine line with no reflection; a dull edge reflects light as a shiny band.
  • In use: the instrument slips over calculus or needs more pressure.

Principles of sharpening dental instruments

Sharpening checklist

  • Sharpen clean, sterilized instruments, or sterilize after sharpening according to protocol.
  • Maintain the original angle between the face and lateral surface (often around 70 to 80 degrees).
  • On Gracey curettes, sharpen only the cutting edge.
  • Finish with light strokes to avoid a wire edge.
  • Keep the toe of curettes rounded.

When to replace instead of sharpen

Each sharpening removes metal. When a blade has become thin, narrow or weak, it can bend or break during use. Replace instruments when the blade has lost a significant amount of its original width.

Frequently asked questions

How often should scalers be sharpened?

Many clinicians sharpen after every few uses. Frequent light sharpening is better than occasional heavy sharpening.

Do I need to sterilize the stone?

Follow your infection control protocol. Many stones can be sterilized; check the manufacturer's instructions.

Can all dental instruments be sharpened?

Scalers, curettes and some surgical instruments can. Explorers and very fine tips are usually replaced.
