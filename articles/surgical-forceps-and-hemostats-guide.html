Surgical Forceps and Hemostats: A Guide to the Common Patterns

"Forceps" covers a large family of instruments that grasp, hold and clamp. Some are held like a pencil and spring open by themselves; others have finger rings and a ratchet that locks them closed. Ordering the right ones starts with knowing which family you need and what the jaw has to do.

In short

Thumb forceps grasp and hold for a moment; ring-handled hemostats and clamps lock in place. Teeth hold firmly but mark tissue; smooth or finely grooved jaws are gentler. Always specify pattern, length and jaw type.

Thumb forceps (tissue and dressing forceps)

Thumb forceps are spring-handled and held between thumb and fingers. They come in two broad types:

  • Dressing forceps have cross-serrated tips with no teeth. They are used to handle dressings, gauze and sutures, and for light handling of tissue.
  • Tissue forceps have teeth at the tip, often described as 1x2 (one tooth on one side fitting between two on the other). Teeth give a secure grip on tougher tissue such as skin and fascia with less pressure.

Common thumb forceps patterns

  • Adson: short, with a broad handle that narrows to fine tips. Used for skin and superficial tissue, with or without teeth.
  • DeBakey: long, slim jaws with fine longitudinal grooves designed to be atraumatic. Widely used for vascular and delicate tissue.
  • Russian: rounded, cup-shaped tips with a serrated rim, useful for grasping tissue firmly.
  • Standard tissue and dressing forceps: straight, general-purpose patterns in several lengths.

Hemostats: clamping vessels

Hemostats have finger rings, a box lock and a ratchet, so they can be clamped on a vessel and left in place. The patterns differ mainly in size and in how much of the jaw is serrated.

Kelly and Crile look almost identical; the quickest way to tell them apart is to look at the jaw. If the serrations stop halfway, it is a Kelly; if they run the full length, it is a Crile.

Grasping and holding forceps

  • Allis: short teeth across the tip that grip tissue firmly. Used where some tissue marking is acceptable.
  • Babcock: broad, fenestrated, rounded jaws without teeth that hold delicate or tubular structures gently.
  • Sponge-holding forceps (for example Foerster): ring-shaped jaws that hold gauze swabs for prepping and blotting.
  • Towel clamps (for example Backhaus): sharp, curved points that hold drapes in place.

What to specify when ordering

Ordering checklist

  • Pattern (for example Adson, DeBakey, Kelly, Mosquito).
  • Length in centimetres or inches.
  • Straight or curved jaws.
  • Jaw type: teeth (and how many), serrated or atraumatic.
  • Insert or finish: standard, tungsten carbide, satin or mirror.
  • Quantity, and whether the items are for a specific set.

Quality points to check

Close the jaws and hold them to the light: the tips should meet exactly and the serrations should mesh along their length. Ratchets on hemostats should engage cleanly and hold on each tooth, and the box lock should move smoothly with no side-to-side play. On thumb forceps, the alignment pin (if present) should guide the tips together, and the spring should feel even and not too stiff.

Caring for forceps

Clean hemostats and clamps open, so the box lock and serrations can be reached by brushes and water. Do not close them past the first ratchet tooth for storage or sterilization, as constant tension can stress the joint over time. Lubricate box locks with a water-soluble instrument lubricant after cleaning.
