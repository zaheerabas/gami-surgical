A needle holder has one job: to hold a curved needle firmly, at the right angle, without letting it twist or slip. When the jaws are wrong for the needle, the needle rotates, bends or gets damaged, and suturing takes longer than it should. The good news is that choosing the right needle holder is mostly a matter of matching it to the sutures you use.

In short

Match the jaw to the needle: fine jaws for fine sutures, heavier jaws for larger needles. Pick the length for the depth of the wound, and consider tungsten carbide inserts for a longer-lasting grip.

Common patterns

Mayo-Hegar

The general-purpose needle holder found in most sets. It has medium-weight jaws, finger rings and a ratchet, and comes in a range of lengths for superficial and deeper work.

Webster

A smaller, lighter needle holder with narrow jaws for fine sutures in delicate or superficial tissue, such as facial and plastic work. Available with smooth or serrated jaws.

Olsen-Hegar

A needle holder with scissors built into the jaws, so one instrument can hold the needle and cut the suture. Popular for minor procedures and in veterinary practice, where working alone is common. Care is needed not to cut the suture by accident while tying.

Crile-Wood and other narrow-jaw patterns

Slimmer jaws for finer needles and better visibility, often used where Mayo-Hegar jaws would be too bulky.

Castroviejo

A spring-handled microsurgical needle holder, held like a pencil and available with or without a lock. Used with very fine sutures in ophthalmic, plastic and microvascular surgery.

Matching the jaw to the suture

The finer the needle, the finer the jaw and the serrations it needs. A jaw that is too heavy crushes or flattens a fine needle; a jaw that is too fine cannot hold a large needle securely and may be damaged by it.

Tungsten carbide inserts

Needle holders with tungsten carbide (TC) inserts are easy to recognise by their gold-coloured handles. Carbide is much harder than steel, so the crosshatched pattern on the jaw holds the needle more securely and wears far more slowly. TC inserts are offered with different serration sizes for different needles, and worn inserts can often be replaced, which extends the instrument's life.

Length

Short needle holders (around 12 to 14 cm) give control in superficial work; longer ones (18 cm and above) reach deeper wounds and body cavities. Many departments keep the same pattern in two or three lengths.

Using a needle holder well

  • Grasp the needle near the tips of the jaws, which gives the most control.
  • Hold the needle roughly between its middle and the back third, away from the point and not on the swaged end where the suture is attached.
  • Close only as far as needed to hold the needle; forcing the ratchet to the last tooth on a fine needle damages both needle and jaws.

Checking and caring for needle holders

Grip test

  • Clamp a needle of the intended size on the first or second ratchet tooth.
  • Try to rotate the needle with your fingers: it should not turn easily.
  • Check the jaws under magnification for worn or flattened serrations.
  • Make sure the ratchet holds on each tooth and releases smoothly.

Clean needle holders in the open position, brushing the serrations and box lock, and lubricate the joint with water-soluble lubricant after cleaning. Send instruments that fail the grip test for repair or insert replacement.
