Lower molars usually have two roots, one towards the front (mesial) and one towards the back (distal). Lower molar forceps have beaks shaped to engage the furcation between these roots on both the cheek and tongue sides, so one pattern works on both left and right.

In short

Lower molar forceps such as No. 17 have pointed beaks on both sides. Cowhorn forceps such as No. 23 have long, curved, pointed beaks that sit in the furcation and can lift the tooth as they close.

How cowhorn forceps work

The two curved, pointed beaks of cowhorn forceps are placed in the furcation, one from the cheek side and one from the tongue side. As the handles are squeezed, the beaks press into the furcation and can lift the tooth upwards. Clinical use requires training and care, because the beaks apply strong force to the bone between the roots.

Lower molars and right-angled beaks

Like other mandibular forceps, lower molar forceps have beaks set at roughly a right angle to the handles, so they can reach down onto the lower teeth.

Quality checklist

Frequently asked questions

Why are lower molar forceps not made in left and right?

Lower molars have roots arranged front and back, with a furcation on both the cheek and tongue sides, so one symmetrical pattern fits both sides.

What is a cowhorn forceps?

A lower molar forceps with long, curved, pointed beaks that look like cow horns, such as the No. 23.

Which lower molar forceps should a clinic stock first?

Many clinics start with a No. 17 and add a cowhorn pattern based on clinician preference.
