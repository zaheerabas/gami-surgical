Passivation is a chemical finishing treatment for stainless steel. It removes free iron and other contaminants left on the surface by machining, grinding and polishing, and helps the steel form a strong protective chromium oxide layer. For instruments that will be cleaned and sterilized hundreds of times, passivation makes a real difference to corrosion resistance.

In short

Passivation cleans the steel surface of free iron and strengthens its natural protective layer. Instruments that are not passivated are more likely to show rust spots early. Standards such as ASTM A967 describe recognised passivation methods.

Why stainless steel needs passivation

Stainless steel resists corrosion because chromium in the steel forms a thin, invisible oxide layer on the surface. During manufacturing, tiny particles of iron from tools and grinding wheels can be pressed into the surface. These particles can rust and interrupt the protective layer, leading to rust spots and pitting.

How passivation works

After cleaning, instruments are immersed in an acid solution, commonly based on nitric acid or citric acid, under controlled conditions. The acid dissolves free iron from the surface without attacking the stainless steel itself. The instruments are then rinsed and dried, and the chromium oxide layer forms more completely.

Standards

ASTM A967 and ASTM A380 are widely used specifications that describe passivation methods and tests. A manufacturer that passivates to a recognised standard can usually explain the method and the tests used.

Frequently asked questions

Can passivation be seen?

No. A passivated instrument looks the same, but it resists corrosion better.

Do instruments lose their passivation?

The protective layer reforms naturally in air, but harsh chemicals, chlorides and damage can weaken it. Correct cleaning and care help it last.

Can hospitals passivate instruments?

Some reprocessing departments and repair services offer passivation treatments for instruments with surface staining. Ask your repair provider.
