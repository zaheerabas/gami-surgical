Luxators and elevators are both used to loosen teeth before extraction, and they can look alike. But they work in different ways. Luxators have thin, sharp blades that cut the periodontal ligament, while elevators are sturdier and are used for leverage. Using a luxator as a lever is the most common way to damage one.

In short

Luxators cut; elevators lever. Work the luxator blade down the root to sever the ligament, then use an elevator or forceps for leverage. Periotomes are even finer instruments for minimal trauma.

How luxators work

A luxator has a thin, flat or gently curved blade with a sharp tip. It is pushed down between the root and the bone with a gentle rocking motion, cutting the periodontal ligament fibres that hold the tooth. As the ligament is cut, the tooth becomes looser, which can make extraction easier and help preserve the surrounding bone.

How elevators work

Elevators have thicker, stronger blades designed to apply leverage, wedging or rotation. They push the tooth or root out of its socket rather than cutting the ligament.

Periotomes

Periotomes have even finer blades and are used to sever the ligament with minimal trauma, especially where bone and gum must be preserved, for example before implant placement.

Looking after luxators

Care checklist

  • Never use a luxator as a lever.
  • Protect the blade in cassettes and during cleaning.
  • Inspect tips for bends and nicks after every use.
  • Have blades resharpened professionally to keep the correct edge.

Frequently asked questions

Can a luxator replace elevators?

Not entirely. Luxators loosen the tooth by cutting the ligament; elevators or forceps are still usually needed to remove it.

Why did my luxator tip bend?

Most often because it was used to lever the tooth. Luxator blades are thin and designed for cutting, not force.

What sizes do luxators come in?

Blade widths commonly range from about 1 mm to 5 mm, straight or curved.
