Allis and Babcock forceps are ring-handled grasping forceps with a ratchet, used to hold tissue during surgery. They often sit next to each other on general and gynaecology trays, but they are designed for different tissue. Allis and Babcock forceps are easy to tell apart once you look at the jaws.

In short

Allis forceps have short teeth that grip firmly and can mark tissue; use them on tougher tissue or tissue that will be removed. Babcock forceps have broad, rounded, fenestrated jaws without teeth; use them to hold delicate or tubular structures gently.

Allis forceps

Allis forceps have jaws that curve slightly towards each other and end in a row of short, interlocking teeth. The jaws only meet at the tip, so tissue is gripped by the teeth over a small area. This gives a secure hold on fascia, tough tissue, or tissue that is going to be excised, but it can damage delicate tissue.

Babcock forceps

Babcock forceps have wide, rounded, fenestrated (open-windowed) jaws with a smooth or finely ridged gripping edge and no teeth. They encircle and lift structures such as bowel, the appendix or fallopian tubes without crushing them.

Sizes

Both patterns are available in several lengths, commonly around 15 to 20 cm, with longer versions for deep work. Allis forceps also come with different numbers of teeth (for example 4x5 or 5x6), which changes the width of the grip.

What to check

Quality checklist

  • Allis: the teeth interlock evenly with no gaps or overlaps.
  • Babcock: the jaws close evenly along the gripping edge without pinching.
  • The ratchet engages cleanly and holds.
  • The box lock is smooth and firm, with no play.

Frequently asked questions

Can Allis forceps be used on bowel?

They are generally avoided on bowel and other delicate tissue because the teeth can injure it. Babcock forceps are the usual choice there.

Are there atraumatic versions of Allis forceps?

Yes. Some patterns, such as Allis-Adair or atraumatic Allis versions, have finer teeth or modified jaws for gentler handling.

Which specialties use these forceps most?

General surgery and gynaecology use both widely, and they appear in many laparotomy and caesarean section sets.
