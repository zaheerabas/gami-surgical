Bone-holding forceps grip bone firmly so the surgeon can position it, hold fragments together, or keep a fracture reduced while it is fixed with plates, screws or wires. They are a core part of orthopaedic and trauma sets.

In short

Bone-holding forceps grip and position bone; reduction forceps hold fracture fragments together. Choose the jaw shape for the bone size and the lock type for how the forceps will be used.

Pointed reduction forceps

Pointed reduction forceps (often called Weber forceps) have two sharp points that grip the bone through small contact areas. They hold fracture fragments together with little soft-tissue disruption, which is valuable when fixing fractures. They come with ratchet or speed locks and in several sizes.

Locking mechanisms

  • Ratchet locks are quick to apply and release.
  • Screw or speed locks allow fine adjustment of grip pressure.
  • Self-centring designs help keep the forceps aligned on round bone.

Choosing the size

Bone-holding forceps are sized to the bone: small patterns for hands, feet and small bones, larger patterns for long bones such as the femur and tibia. Trauma sets often include several sizes of the same pattern.

Quality checklist

  • The jaws close symmetrically and the teeth or points meet correctly.
  • The lock holds firmly under load and releases smoothly.
  • The joint is solid with no play.
  • The instrument is heavy enough to hold bone without flexing.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between bone-holding and reduction forceps?

Bone-holding forceps grip a bone to move or hold it. Reduction forceps are designed to bring fracture fragments together and keep them aligned.

Why do pointed reduction forceps have sharp points?

The points grip the bone through small contact areas, so the forceps hold firmly while disturbing less soft tissue.

How should they be cleaned?

Clean them open, brushing teeth, points and locks, and check that the lock still holds before sterilizing.
